A Pilgrim's Journey: The Autobiography of Ignatius of Loyola

Central argument

Ignatius, by then superior general of the Society of Jesus, dictated this account of his life between 1553 and 1555 to Luís Gonçalves da Câmara, from the wound at Pamplona (1521) to his arrival in Rome (1538). He speaks of himself in the third person and calls himself "the pilgrim". The book tells less about deeds than about the way God taught him, "come un maestro di scuola tratta un bambino" (the way a schoolmaster treats a child): through mistakes, excesses and corrections. Ignatius learned to recognise God's will by watching what his thoughts left behind in him, before he could read Latin or had any rule. The Spiritual Exercises come from that experience; he tried them on himself before giving them to others.

Examples / Notable passages

  • The convalescence at Loyola. There are no chivalric romances in the house, only a life of Christ and the lives of the saints. Ignatius alternates daydreams of glory and of a lady with the thought of doing what Saint Francis and Saint Dominic did, and notices that the first leave him "secco e scontento" (dry and discontented), the second content even afterwards. It's the first rule of the discernment of spirits, found by looking at the after.
  • The mule and the Moor. On the road to Montserrat he argues with a Moor about Mary's virginity; when the man rides off, he can't decide whether to go after him and kill him. He lets the mule decide: if it takes the Moor's road, he'll follow. The mule goes the other way. Ignatius tells it as proof of how blind he still was.
  • The scruples at Manresa. He confesses the same sins over and over, gets to the point of thinking of throwing himself out of a window, and fasts a whole week until his confessor stops him. He gets out of it when he decides not to confess his past faults any more: he understands that the restlessness came from the enemy, not from God.
  • The Cardoner. Sitting on the bank of the river "gli si cominciarono ad aprire gli occhi dell'intelletto" (the eyes of his understanding began to open): he says he learned more there than in all the rest of his life.
  • Jerusalem. He wants to stay in the Holy Land for good; the Franciscan custodian orders him to leave, and he obeys. Back in Barcelona, past thirty, he sits down among schoolboys to study Latin grammar.
  • The Inquisition. In Alcalá and in Salamanca he ends up in prison, in Salamanca chained by one foot. He tells his visitors that for the love of God he would wear even more chains. He is acquitted, but forbidden to teach until he has studied.
  • La Storta. Near Rome, in 1537, he has the vision of the Father placing him with the Son: the point where the "pilgrim" becomes the founder.

Objections

  • It's an account dictated thirty years after the events by a man who has already founded an order and knows how things turned out: the life is arranged like a school, and the mistakes become lessons. Câmara also wrote from memory, sometimes days after the conversations.
  • It stops in 1538. There is almost nothing about the Society, the Constitutions or the last eighteen years of governing: the book explains how discernment was born, not how he used it as a superior.
  • The penances at Manresa (fasting, hair and nails left to grow, seven hours of prayer on his knees) are told without much distance. The book presents them as an excess he was healed of, but for a reader today the line between asceticism and scrupulosity isn't always clear.
