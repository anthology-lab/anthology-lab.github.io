The Case for Christ

Immediate reaction

The book works better as a structure than as a closed argument. The "skeptical journalist questions the expert" setup is good at holding very different kinds of evidence (manuscripts, forensic medicine, archaeology, clinical psychology, philosophy) inside one logical frame. But it's also a convenient frame for Strobel, because every chapter is built to end with him convinced: there's never any doubt about how the interview will go.

Ideas that stuck with me

  • The distinction between "creed believed" and "creed received/delivered" in 1 Corinthians 15:3-8 (Ch. 1, Blomberg). The technical rabbinic language of transmission, together with Paul's visit to Jerusalem (Galatians 1:18-19, historeo, "to investigate in person"), puts the core of the Easter faith 2 to 8 years after the crucifixion. That's too early for a legend to form and replace the facts.
  • The criterion of embarrassment (Ch. 2, Blomberg). Women as the first witnesses of the empty tomb is a detail no first-century inventor would have chosen, because a woman's testimony had no legal weight in that culture. The same goes for the diverging genealogies, the disciples' failures, Jesus' anguish in Gethsemane.
  • The numbers on manuscripts (Ch. 3, Metzger): more than 5,000 Greek manuscripts of the New Testament against a single manuscript of Tacitus (copied in AD 850, about 750 years later) or the nine manuscripts of Josephus (11th-12th century). The fragment of John 18, dated AD 100-150 by C. H. Roberts, is enough on its own to sink the 19th-century German thesis of a late fourth gospel (AD 160).
  • Peter Stoner's probability calculation (Ch. 10, Lapides, and again in the Conclusion): 1 in 10^17 that one man would fulfil by chance even just 8 specific messianic prophecies.
  • C.S. Lewis's trilemma (lunatic/liar/Lord), quoted explicitly in the Conclusion as the book's rhetorical capstone.
  • Julius Müller's open challenge of 1844 (Conclusion): Müller publicly challenged anyone to produce a single historical example of a legend that formed and spread within two generations. According to Strobel, nobody took it up in the 150 years that followed.
  • Boyd's chapter on the Jesus Seminar (Ch. 6) is the only place where Strobel really stages a confrontation with a hostile academic position. He doesn't just quote it to refute it from a distance; he has Boyd explain why he thinks its method is flawed (a small, unrepresentative group, a naturalist presupposition that rules out miracles in advance, majority voting as a pseudo-scientific procedure).

Confusing, irritating or weak parts

  • Non-adversarial choice of witnesses: all thirteen interviewees are evangelical scholars already convinced of the thesis they're supposed to "prove". There's never a real cross-examination with a skeptic defending their own position in the first person. The partial exception is Ch. 6, where Boyd reports and refutes the Jesus Seminar's position, but always through his own voice, never in direct dialogue with a member of the Seminar.
  • The legal/journalistic frame as a built-in bias: the "trial" setup makes every piece of evidence look like it's building a one-way case towards a verdict, when in fact the choice of questions and experts is already pointed at the outcome.
  • Rhetorical overconfidence: Strobel ends almost every chapter with his own surrender ("I was stunned", "I had no comeback"). It's a recurring narrative device, and it makes it hard to tell the real strength of the argument from the author's dramatic staging.
  • Limits the book itself admits (Ch. 5, McRay): the dating of Quirinius's census (Luke 2:2) and the lack of extra-biblical confirmation for the massacre of the innocents (Matthew 2) stay uncovered. McRay offers ways to harmonize them, but Strobel himself doesn't present them as fully solved, unlike the triumphant tone he uses elsewhere.
  • Moreland's fifth piece of circumstantial evidence (Ch. 14, the church's survival despite persecution) is explicitly flagged by Moreland himself as the weakest of the five, a rare moment of self-criticism inside the book.

The case, objection by objection

A table built chapter by chapter: on the left the skeptical/atheist objection the chapter deals with, on the right the evidence and arguments the book actually presents. Nothing below is made up: every quote and figure can be traced to the text.

Objection (the atheist's doubt) Chapter · interviewee Evidence and arguments (from the book)
The gospels are anonymous and late: they weren't written by eyewitnesses, so their "biography of Jesus" is unreliable at the source Ch. 1 · Craig Blomberg The authorship tradition going back to Papias and Irenaeus (Mark as Peter's interpreter, Luke as Paul's companion); the creed of 1 Corinthians 15:3-8 dated 2 to 8 years after the crucifixion through the technical rabbinic language of "received/delivered"; the Aramaic name "Cephas"; Paul's investigative visit to Jerusalem (Galatians 1:18-19); Thomas's final confession ("My Lord and my God!") as the peak of a high Christology already present in the earliest texts
The gospels contain contradictions that can't be reconciled, and invented or legendary details Ch. 2 · Craig Blomberg The "eight tests" of historical reliability (intention, ability, character, consistency, bias, cover-up, corroboration, adverse witness); the centurion's story (Matthew/Luke) as complementary perspectives, not contradictory ones; the Gerasenes/Gadarenes question explained by regional naming; the diverging genealogies reconciled as legal line (Joseph) vs blood line (Mary); the criterion of embarrassment (women as first witnesses, the disciples' failures, Jesus' anguish), details a forger wouldn't have invented
We only have copies of copies of copies, full of copying errors: the text we read today doesn't match the original Ch. 3 · Bruce Metzger More than 5,000 Greek manuscripts of the NT against the single manuscript of Tacitus (copied in AD 850) or the nine manuscripts of Josephus (11th-12th century); a gap of about 100-150 years between original and first copy against 700-1,400 years for other ancient texts; the papyrus fragment of John 18 dated AD 100-150 by C. H. Roberts; an estimate of 99.5% textual purity (Geisler/Nix); no church doctrine is at stake in the existing variants
The NT canon was manipulated for political reasons: "alternative" gospels like Thomas were censored for doctrinal reasons Ch. 3 · Bruce Metzger The Gospel of Thomas, dated around AD 140, has no apostolic authority and contains pantheistic ("split a piece of wood, and I am there") and misogynistic elements foreign to the canonical gospels; for Metzger it "excluded itself". The three historical criteria (apostolic authority, conformity to the rule of faith, continuous use in the church) were applied with broad agreement already in the first two centuries, not decided at a table by a council
There are no independent historical sources outside the Bible that confirm Jesus existed or what he claimed Ch. 4 · Edwin Yamauchi Josephus (Jewish Antiquities, the reference to "James, the brother of Jesus who was called Christ", and the Testimonium Flavianum); Tacitus (Annals 15.44, the execution of "Christus" under Pontius Pilate); Pliny the Younger's letter to Trajan on Christian worship; Thallus/Julius Africanus on the darkness at the crucifixion; references in the Talmud
Archaeology doesn't confirm (or contradicts) the geographical and historical details of the gospels Ch. 5 · John McRay Luke's documented accuracy on official titles, geography and small details, confirmed by archaeological finds; limits admitted in the book itself: the dating of Quirinius's census (Luke 2:2) and the lack of extra-biblical confirmation for the massacre of the innocents (Matthew 2) remain open problems, with hypotheses for harmonizing them but no final answer
The Jesus Seminar (scholars voting with coloured beads) showed that most of the sayings and deeds attributed to Jesus aren't authentic Ch. 6 · Gregory Boyd A direct critique of method: a small, unrepresentative group (about 74 scholars, mostly skeptical from the start), a naturalist presupposition that rules out miracles in advance, a majority-vote procedure that isn't scientific; the Seminar's conclusions are rejected by most New Testament scholarship
Jesus never claimed to be God: the church invented his divinity later Ch. 7 · Ben Witherington III Implicit claims through the title "Son of Man" linked to Daniel 7:13-14 (a divine figure who receives dominion and worship); actions that assume divine authority (forgiving sins, authority over the Sabbath, accepting worship) as an implicit Christology, not only explicit statements
Jesus was deluded or a megalomaniac, suffering from a psychiatric disorder when he claimed to be God Ch. 8 · Gary Collins A clinical analysis against the standard diagnostic criteria for narcissism, psychosis and delusion: none of the typical symptoms (irrationality, inappropriate social behaviour, incongruous emotions, poor relationships); instead, calm under pressure, clear answers, deep compassion, consistent teaching
The idea of a God who becomes man (the incarnation) is philosophically incoherent or contradictory Ch. 9 · D.A. Carson The theology of kenosis (Philippians 2, the "emptying"): the divine attributes are voluntarily veiled, not destroyed, during the incarnation. A coherent picture within its own theological frame, with answers to the objections about limited omniscience and omnipotence
Jesus planned the fulfilment of the messianic prophecies himself, or it's pure coincidence Ch. 10 · Louis Lapides Prophecies outside Jesus' human control: place of birth (Micah 5:2), manner of death (Psalm 22, Isaiah 53), the price of the betrayal (Zechariah 11:12-13), no bone broken (Psalm 34:20); Peter Stoner's probability calculation (1 in 10^17 for just 8 prophecies); Lapides's own conversion story, a Jew who set out to disprove Jesus' messiahship through the prophecies and ended up convinced by them
Jesus didn't really die on the cross: he fainted and was revived in the cool tomb (the swoon theory) Ch. 11 · Alexander Metherell Detailed medical mechanics: hematidrosis from stress in Gethsemane, flogging with the flagrum and massive blood loss, the mechanics of crucifixion with hypovolemic shock and progressive asphyxiation, the spear thrust releasing "blood and water" (pericardial/pleural effusion) as clinical proof that death had already happened, the professional certainty of the Roman soldiers (they would have been executed themselves for letting a condemned man survive)
The disciples (or someone else) stole the body to stage the resurrection / the empty tomb is a later legend Ch. 12 · William Lane Craig The burial by Joseph of Arimathea (an implausible invention: making up a member of the Sanhedrin as the honourable burier makes no sense as propaganda); women as the first witnesses (criterion of embarrassment, given their status as legally invalid witnesses); the Jewish "guard story", examined and set aside by Craig himself as weak evidence; a method for harmonizing contradictions (historian vs philosopher, secondary details vs the core); the wrong-tomb theory (Kirsopp Lake) and the legend theory, both rejected
The appearances of the risen Jesus were hallucinations or projections of the disciples' wishes Ch. 13 · Gary Habermas Hallucinations are individual events, not collective and simultaneous (the direct clinical opinion of psychologist Gary Collins); the "more than five hundred brothers at the same time" (1 Corinthians 15); nine distinct appearances listed in the gospels and Acts, to different people in different settings; the conversion of declared skeptics (not only of people who already believed); the missing ending of Mark (16:9-20) judged not to harm the overall case
Even granting the single facts, no direct and final proof shows the resurrection happened: it could be religious enthusiasm with no real event behind it Ch. 14 · J.P. Moreland Five pieces of circumstantial evidence: the disciples' willing martyrdom for a fact they say they knew, not just believed; the conversion of declared skeptics like James and Paul (with the counter-objection of Muhammad's conversion explicitly raised and discussed); the abandonment, within a few weeks, of five deep-rooted Jewish social and religious structures; the appearance of unusual rites (Communion, baptism) commemorating a shameful execution; the church's survival and growth despite persecution, flagged by Moreland himself as the weakest of the five

Beyond this book: material NOT in this text

Two groups of arguments often linked to Strobel don't appear in The Case for Christ. They're in other books of the same series (The Case for Faith, The Case for a Creator), which deal explicitly with science and the origin of life, and with theodicy and religious pluralism. This book doesn't cover those topics: it stays on the identity and resurrection of Jesus.

Not in this book (science, miracles, origin of life): the objection that "evolution and chance explain the origin of life with no need for God", the Miller-Urey experiment, DNA complexity and the Intelligent Design argument. Typical material of The Case for a Creator, not found here.

Not in this book (Christian exclusivity, suffering, relativism): the objection that Christian exclusivism is intolerance, religious pluralism, and the problem of evil and suffering. Typical material of The Case for Faith.

What the book does include is C.S. Lewis's trilemma, in the Conclusion, and Stoner's calculation on the prophecies, in Ch. 10 (Lapides) and again in the Conclusion.

Central argument

The book argues that the resurrection of Jesus holds up to a historical and legal standard of proof, not just to blind faith, once you combine documentary evidence (manuscripts, early dating of the creeds), medical evidence (certain death on the cross), circumstantial evidence (the disciples' behaviour) and philosophical evidence (the coherence of the incarnation, Lewis's trilemma). The Conclusion itself sums up this cumulative argument: no single piece of evidence settles it alone, but the convergence of independent evidence (documentary + medical + circumstantial + prophetic) makes the naturalistic explanations (legend, theft, hallucination, swoon), according to Strobel, less economical than a real resurrection. Sherwin-White's research on the minimum time legends needed to form in the ancient world, and Julius Müller's open challenge (1844, never taken up), are the logical hinge of the cumulative case.

Objections

Limits the book itself admits, separate from the atheist objections dealt with in the table above:

  • The dating of Quirinius's census (Luke 2:2): in Ch. 5 McRay offers hypotheses but doesn't settle the question with the confidence used elsewhere.
  • The lack of extra-biblical confirmation for the massacre of the innocents (Matthew 2): same chapter, same admitted margin of uncertainty.
  • Moreland's fifth piece of circumstantial evidence (the church's survival despite persecution) is explicitly called the weakest of the five, a rare case of self-criticism inside the book.
  • The method of the book itself: no witness is really hostile to the thesis they have to support (except, partly, Boyd's account of the Jesus Seminar). It's a limit of the whole argument, not of one chapter.

Examples / Notable passages

  • The papyrus fragment of John 18 (Ch. 3), which pushes the fourth gospel back to AD 100-150 and disproves the 19th-century thesis of a late composition.
  • Peter Stoner's calculation on the messianic prophecies (Ch. 10 and Conclusion).
  • Metherell's medical account of death by crucifixion (Ch. 11), the most technically detailed chapter in the book.
  • Habermas's personal story "The Resurrection of Debbie" (Ch. 13).
  • C.S. Lewis's trilemma in the Conclusion, as the final rhetorical summary.
