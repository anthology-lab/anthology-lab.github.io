Other Inquisitions, 1937-1952

Central argument

Altre inquisizioni (Otras inquisiciones) is a collection of essays Borges published in 1952, where he uses literature as a tool for philosophy and philosophy as material for literature. The thread running through the whole book is identity: of people, of ideas, of time itself. Borges never argues head-on. He builds labyrinths of quotations, anecdotes and paradoxes, and lets the reader get lost in them. The implicit thesis is that every concept is a metaphor for another, every author is also his own precursors, every moment contains all moments.

Examples / Notable passages

  • "Kafka and His Precursors." Borges shows that a great author creates his own precursors after the fact: after Kafka, certain texts by Browning, Kierkegaard and Han Yu read as Kafkaesque. Literary descent runs against time.
  • "Pascal's Sphere." He follows a single image (the sphere whose centre is everywhere and whose circumference is nowhere) through the centuries, from theology to physics: every age reinvented the same fear of the infinite.
  • "A New Refutation of Time." A philosophical argument that time doesn't exist: if two identical moments are identical, they are the same moment. But the essay ends by refuting itself: "Time is the substance of which I am made [...] The world, unfortunately, is real; I, unfortunately, am Borges". Denying time, he says, is a consolation that doesn't hold.

Synthesis

Borges builds a cosmology made of texts. Every book points to endless other books; every idea has already been thought elsewhere and in another form; the canon is a labyrinth, not a line. Reading Altre inquisizioni means accepting that you won't leave the library, and finding in that a form of the infinite rather than a limit.

Objections

  • The dense erudition can turn into a self-referential game: Borges quotes what he has read, but the line between learning and invention is left porous on purpose.
  • Paradox as a method can wear out: after a while the reader wonders whether there is a thesis or only a performance of wonder.
  • Borges's ironic detachment leaves emotion out: these essays think a great deal, but they feel almost nothing.
Highlights
  • Jacket copy: "è proprio questa la peculiarità del libro: dopo di esso, la parola letteratura ha assunto un significato che prima non aveva, estendendosi su tutto come un velo leggerissimo."
  • Pascal's Sphere: "the French theologian Alain de Lille (Alanus de Insulis) discovered, at the end of the twelfth century, the following formula, which future ages would not forget: 'God is an intelligible sphere, whose center is everywhere and whose circumference is nowhere.'"
  • The Flower of Coleridge: "The first, by Coleridge—I am not sure if he wrote it at the end of the eighteenth or beginning of the nineteenth century—says: 'If a man could pass through Paradise in a dream, and have a flower presented to him as a pledge that his soul had really been there, and if he found that flower in his hand when he awoke—Ay!—and what then?'"
  • Time and J. W. Dunne: "The seventh of India's many philosophical systems recorded by Paul Deussen (Nachvedische Philosophie der Inder, 318) denies the self as an immediate object of knowledge, 'because if our soul were knowable, a second soul would be required to know the first and a third to know the second.' The Hindus have no sense of history (they stubbornly prefer to examine ideas rather than the names and dates of philosophers), but we know that this radical negation of introspection is about eight centuries old."
  • A New Refutation of Time: "if time is a mental process, how can myriads of men, or even two distinct men, share it at all?"
  • A New Refutation of Time: "Such is Bernard Shaw's judgment (Guide to Socialism, 86): 'What you can suffer is the maximum that can be suffered on earth. If you die of starvation, you will suffer all the starvation there has been or will be. If ten thousand people die with you, their participation in your lot will not make you be ten thousand times more hungry nor multiply the time of your agony ten thousand times. Do not let yourself be overcome by the horrible sum of human sufferings; such a sum does not exist. Neither poverty nor pain are cumulative.' (Cf. also The Problem of Pain, VII, by C. S. Lewis.)"
  • A New Refutation of Time: "'The man in the past, of the past moment,' the Path to Purity advises us, 'has lived, but he does not live, nor will he live; the man of a future moment will live, but he has not lived nor does he now live; the man of the present moment lives, but he has not lived nor will he live'"
  • A New Refutation of Time, ending: "Our destiny (in contrast to Swedenborg's hell and the hell of Tibetan mythology) is not frightful because it is unreal; it is frightful because it is irreversible and ironbound. Time is the substance of which I am made. Time is a river which sweeps me along, but I am the river; it is a tiger which mangles me, but I am the tiger; it is a fire which consumes me, but I am the fire. The world, unfortunately, is real; I, unfortunately, am Borges."
