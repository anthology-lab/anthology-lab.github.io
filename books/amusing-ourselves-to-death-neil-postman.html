Amusing Ourselves to Death: Public Discourse in the Age of Show Business

Central argument

Neil Postman wrote the book in 1985, about American television, and opens it with a comparison between two dystopias: Nineteen Eighty-Four, where truth is hidden from people, and Brave New World, where truth drowns in a sea of irrelevance and nobody minds. His bet is on Huxley. The danger he sees is not a censor but the entertainment we choose freely.

The thesis rests on a media-ecology idea he takes from McLuhan and pushes further: the form of a medium decides what content can fit in it. Every culture accepts truth only in certain forms, and its main medium shapes which ones. Print trained people to follow long, connected arguments; television works with images, speed and feeling, so on TV everything (news, politics, religion, school) is presented as a show. Postman has nothing against entertainment as entertainment. His worry is serious discourse turned into entertainment without anyone noticing, until a population is well informed and understands less.

The ideas from the book I kept:

  • You can't do philosophy with smoke. Smoke signals can't carry philosophy, and television can't carry a long argument. On TV, politics rewards looks and quick lines more than reasoning. When a video or a post leaves me with a feeling but no argument, the format may be the reason.
  • Why numbers feel truer than stories. An oral culture trusts proverbs, a university trusts the printed page, today we trust numbers. Before dismissing a claim, it's worth asking whether it's actually weak or only arrived in an unfamiliar form, a story instead of a chart.
  • The crowd that listened for seven hours. In 1854, at Peoria, Lincoln and Douglas spoke for about seven hours in total, and the crowd stayed; the 1858 debates ran three hours each. Postman credits print: a reading culture trains people to follow long reasoning. That stamina is a habit, and the media you spend your days with build it or wear it down.
  • Facts you can't do anything with. The telegraph flooded people with news from far away that they could do nothing about. Postman calls it a shift in the information-action ratio: you learn more and more and can act on less and less. Much daily news still works this way. Information is only as useful as what it lets you do.
  • The trusted face trap. On television, credibility means seeming sincere, not having a record of telling the truth. Newscasts jump between unrelated stories with "Now... this", so nothing connects. The result is what Postman calls disinformation: facts that leave you feeling informed while you understand less. When a calm face makes a claim convincing, check the claim.
  • Ads that sell you yourself. TV commercials rarely say anything about the product; they show the kind of person you'd like to be. Politicians learned the same trick, an image you can see yourself in, which says nothing about how they would govern. When an ad or a candidate makes me feel understood, the question is what I actually learned.
  • When learning has to be fun. Educational television like Sesame Street follows three rules: no prerequisites, no perplexity, no exposition. Postman says this turns learning into entertainment, and children learn to love television, not school. Real understanding needs sequence, effort and some confusion: when a subject gets hard, I've reached the part a screen would skip.
  • When everything has to entertain. The same happens to religion: a sermon on TV has to compete with everything else on the screen, so it becomes a performance with the preacher as the star.
  • Questions to ask any screen. Postman's remedy is not to switch off but a habit of asking questions about any medium: what kind of thinking it rewards, what it ignores, what it asks of me. In his introduction to the 20th anniversary edition his son Andrew argues the questions fit any new technology too. Huxley's warning was about people laughing without knowing why; asking is how you find out.

Objections

  • Postman compares the best of print culture (the Lincoln-Douglas debates, the sermons of the Great Awakening) with the worst of television. Print also carried pamphlets, sensational newspapers and propaganda; the age of reason he describes was narrower than the book suggests.
  • The remedy is thin. After 150 pages of diagnosis, a habit of questions and a school that teaches about media feel small next to the force he describes.
  • The book is about television, and some of it has aged: the evening news has lost its central place. The core idea works better today than the examples do. Short video and the feed fit his description more closely than the 1985 newscast did.
