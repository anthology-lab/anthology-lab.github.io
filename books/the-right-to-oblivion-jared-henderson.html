The Right to Oblivion

Central argument

Privacy is not about protecting information that already exists but about keeping a space of non-fixity: a biographical ambiguity where there is no "fact of the matter" yet. Destroying it harms autonomy and the capacity to change.

Analysis

Pressly separates secrecy (protecting defined information) from oblivion (the absence of information: a state of ambiguity and potential that is destroyed the moment something is turned into data). Privacy is not about what to hide but about producing this undefined space. The harm of a violation doesn't require the victim to know about it: Pressly argues that it lies in the loss of biographical possibility, the loss of the zone of non-accountability every human life needs, the space where we don't have to justify ourselves to anyone. The chapter on memory and forgetting brings the thesis down to personal ethics: too much fixity, in our public identity as in our own memories, is a moral harm, because it sends the message that change is useless. Finally, Pressly argues that oblivion produces human depth in three ways: through sleep (meeting the limits of our agency), through the private sphere as shelter for the growth of the self, and through resistance to full articulation, the condition of "round" characters, and of deep human beings.

About the author

Lowry Pressly is a philosopher. The Right to Oblivion is the book read in the philosophy of technology book club run by Jared Henderson (Commonplace Philosophy, 2026). The book enters the debate on digital privacy as an alternative to purely informational views.

Line of argument

↳ Thesis: privacy is the space of non-fixity that comes before any information: not what we hide, but what hasn't been turned into data yet.
↳ Argument (Part 1): the secrecy/oblivion distinction; the Svenson case, where the photograph destroys the ambiguity the gaze had left intact; Warren and Brandeis as the founders of the problem.
↳ Argument (Part 2): harm without damage: Pressly answers the voyeur paradox. The harm is the loss of biographical possibility and of the zone of non-accountability, whether or not anyone finds out.
↳ Argument (Part 3): personal ethics: being fixed to the past is a moral harm ("shackled is bad for the message it sends that it is futile even to try"); Henderson goes further: carrying your own transgressions forever is immoral toward yourself.
↳ Argument (Part 4): oblivion as a producer of depth: sleep, the private sphere, resistance to full articulation; privacy produces social trust.
↳ Conclusion: a regime of total visibility is not only invasive: it produces flat people, unable to grow, unable to trust.

Reflection

Connection to values

The right to oblivion connects directly to growing through consistency, not perfection: being fixed to the past sends the message that change is useless, which is exactly what Pressly calls a moral harm. Non-fixity is the structural condition that lets every imperfect day be a piece of data and not a permanent sentence.
