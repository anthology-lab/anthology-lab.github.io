Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning

Central argument

The book puts together a writer, Peter Brown, and two cognitive psychologists from Washington University in St. Louis, Henry Roediger and Mark McDaniel, who spent their careers studying memory. Its claim is that most people study in the ways that feel best and work worst: rereading, highlighting, cramming, practising one thing over and over until it runs smoothly. Those methods produce fluency with a familiar page, and fluency is easy to mistake for knowledge.

What works feels slower and harder: pulling things back from memory instead of looking at them again, leaving gaps between sessions, mixing different kinds of problems, trying to answer before being shown how. Psychologist Robert Bjork calls these desirable difficulties: the effort of retrieving is what strengthens the memory. So the feeling that a study session went well is a poor guide; a better test is what I can remember or do without help.

The ideas from the book I kept:

  • Retrieval beats rereading. Most study time goes into putting information in front of your eyes, but learning depends on being able to bring it back from memory. A closed book gives a much more honest picture of what you know than a familiar page. The research name is the testing effect: a short quiz with nothing at stake teaches more than another reading.
  • A little forgetting helps you learn. A spaced schedule feels less effective than cramming because some of the material fades between sessions. That fading is part of why it works: the extra effort to bring it back is what strengthens the memory (spacing effect).
  • Mixed practice beats drilling. Practising one method at a time makes it easy to know what to do next, but real problems rarely come with the method attached. A large part of any skill is recognising which approach a situation calls for, and interleaving different problems trains that choice as well as the use.
  • The struggle builds the memory. Ease can make a session feel successful without being a sign of learning. Some difficulty is useful because it forces you to work with what you know rather than just recognise what is in front of you.
  • A wrong guess still teaches you. Jumping straight to the answer skips trying to work it out first. Even a wrong guess gets you thinking about the problem before you see the solution, and the correction becomes easier to remember (generation effect).
  • Your learning style is a myth. There is no good evidence that teaching people in their preferred style ("visual", "auditory", "verbal") helps them learn more (learning styles). A fixed idea of yourself as one kind of learner can keep you away from methods that would work. The method should depend on what you're learning.
  • Effort rewires your brain. Early frustration feels like proof that you're not good at something. Difficulty is often part of building a new skill while the brain is still adapting to it, and with practice what once took a lot of concentration gets easier. The book uses Carol Dweck's research on growth mindset here: ability is not fixed.

Objections

  • Most of the research behind the book comes from lab and classroom studies with word lists, facts and exam material. It's convincing for things you can test; it says less about understanding a hard text, a craft, or slow changes in judgment, where there is no clear "right answer" to retrieve.
  • The book is written for students, teachers and trainers, and it keeps the frame of performance: exams, grades, professional skills. Learning as something you do for its own sake, or as attention to what you're reading, stays outside it.
  • The stories (pilots, surgeons, Marines) are there to make the science stick, and some chapters repeat the same few principles many times. The core fits in a page, which is also proof that the book practises spaced repetition on its reader.
