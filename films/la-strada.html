La Strada

this note talks about the whole film, ending included.

What it's about

Italy, the 1950s. Gelsomina's mother (Gelsomina is a young woman with a simple, almost childlike mind) sells her for 10,000 lire to Zampanò, a brutal travelling showman who makes his living with a strongman act: he breaks an iron chain wrapped around his chest by swelling his muscles. Gelsomina is the second girl he has bought this way: the first, her sister Rosa, died in circumstances the film never explains.

With Zampanò. Gelsomina learns to play the bass drum, to blow the trumpet, to do little clown routines to warm up the crowd before his act. Zampanò uses her, beats her, leaves her at night to sleep with other women, then comes back as if nothing had happened. She stays, not for lack of will, but out of an instinctive loyalty she can't explain even to herself.

The circus and Il Matto. When they join a small circus, Gelsomina meets Il Matto ("the Fool"): a tightrope walker, clown and jester who sees something in her that Zampanò can't see. The two men hate each other more deeply than an ordinary quarrel: Il Matto sees through Zampanò, and that is unforgivable.

The pebble scene. Gelsomina is in complete despair. She tells Il Matto she's no use to anyone and is tired of living. He picks up an ordinary stone and points out that even that stone is good for something. He doesn't know what (if he knew, he'd be the Eternal Father), but it must be, because if a stone is useless then everything is useless, even the stars. "E anche tu, con la testa di carciofo, servi a qualcosa." ("And you too, with your artichoke head, are good for something.") Gelsomina takes the stone. She decides to stay with Zampanò, because if she goes, who will stay with him?

This scene is the structural and philosophical hinge of the film. The decision Gelsomina makes here sets up everything that follows.

The death of Il Matto. Zampanò's anger at Il Matto explodes in a fight on a deserted road. He beats him, and this time Il Matto dies under the blows. It isn't clear whether Zampanò meant to kill him: it's his ordinary excess that, for once, has final consequences. He hides the body, gets back in the van and drives off.

Gelsomina's collapse. Il Matto's death breaks Gelsomina. She can't come out of it: she stays in a dissociated state and only repeats the sad trumpet melody she and Il Matto shared. Zampanò can't face her, not because he doesn't care, but because he has no tools to do it. One night he leaves her asleep by the side of a road.

The death offscreen. Years later. Zampanò is still on the road, still doing his act. He meets a woman hanging out the washing: the strange girl who used to live there, always sad, always playing a tune, has died. Someone had found her and looked after her, and she died like that, in silence. The tune the woman whistles tells him who it was: Gelsomina.

The ending on the beach. The news hits Zampanò like a delayed explosion. That night, after getting drunk and picking a fight in a bar, declaring "I don't need anybody", he drags himself to the sea. Alone on the beach, he breaks down. He doesn't just cry: he's destroyed. He falls to his knees and then onto the sand, shaken by sobs he can neither stop nor understand. It's the first moment of real humanity we see in him, and it comes when there's no one left to receive it. The film ends there.

What it triggered

Before watching, starting from the pebble scene I'd already seen as a reel:

The pebble argument doesn't answer the question "what are you for?": it refuses to answer and replaces the question. It isn't consolation, it's a logical move. If you can prove the stone is useless, you prove everything is useless. The reductio ad absurdum climbs up to the stars, and so the burden of proof flips. → Io sono ignorante, ma ho letto qualche libro. Tu non ci crederai, ma tutto quello che c'è a questo mondo serve a qualcosa. Ecco, prendi quel sasso lì, per esempio. Quale? Questo, uno qualunque. Beh, anche questo serve a qualcosa, anche questo sassetto. E a cosa serve? Serve... Ma che ne so io? Se lo sapessi sai chi sarei? Chi? Il padre eterno che sa tutto. Quando nasci, quando mori. E chi può saperlo? No. Non lo so a cosa serve questo sasso lì. Ma a qualcosa deve servire. Perché se questo è inutile, allora è inutile tutto. Anche le stelle.

The mechanism of the ending is the one of all classical tragedy: Zampanò sees Gelsomina's worth only when she's gone. The ability to receive love opens at the exact moment its object is lost. It's no accident that the film ends at the sea, the same place where it began.

Free notes

Nino Rota and the melody. The musical theme tied to Gelsomina, a sad circus tune played on the trumpet, is one of the most recognisable film compositions of the 20th century. It's the sign of her presence and the sign of her loss: when the woman hanging out the washing whistles it, Zampanò understands everything from that tune before he understands it in words.

The neorealist controversy. When it came out in Italy, the film was attacked hard by Marxist critics, led by Guido Aristarco of Cinema Nuovo. The charge: betraying neorealism, falling back into pre-fascist mysticism, individualism, "i più sottili veleni della letteratura prebellica" ("the subtlest poisons of pre-war literature"). Fellini had left class analysis behind for Catholic spirituality and personal myth, and that was unforgivable. André Bazin defended the film from France, warning about the intolerance Marxist criticism was showing towards Rossellini and Fellini. Fellini said the film came from watching his parents' marriage.

The first Oscar for a foreign film. It won the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film in 1957, at the 29th ceremony, the first year the award was a competitive category (before that it was honorary). The producers who received it were De Laurentiis and Ponti.

Pope Francis. La strada is Francis's favourite film. He saw it for the first time as a young man in Buenos Aires, the year before he entered the seminary. In a video message for the film's 70th anniversary (Rimini, May 2024): "Il film comincia con le lacrime e finisce con le lacrime; inizia in riva al mare e finisce in riva al mare. Ma soprattutto la scena del matto con il sassolino mi è rimasta nel cuore, che dà senso alla vita di quella ragazza."
"The film begins with tears and ends with tears; it begins by the sea and ends by the sea. But above all the scene of the fool with the pebble stayed in my heart, the one that gives meaning to that girl's life."

In an Easter homily in 2017 he had used the image: "E con la fede nel Cristo risorto, anche noi, sassolini su questa terra di dolore e di tragedia, acquisiamo un senso."
"And with faith in the risen Christ, we too, little pebbles on this earth of pain and tragedy, find a meaning."

In the pebble scene he saw a Franciscan echo: Gelsomina as the poverello, grace in the humble and the seemingly useless.

Gelsomina and the figure of the fool. Richard Basehart as Il Matto is the classic Shakespearean fool: the only one who sees the truth and says it, the only one who recognises who Gelsomina really is. His death isn't just a murder: it's the destruction of the witness, the only one who had named her worth.
